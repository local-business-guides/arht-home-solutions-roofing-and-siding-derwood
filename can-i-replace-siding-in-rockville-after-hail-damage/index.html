What should siding replacement after hail damage include?

A proper job starts with an inspection, not an automatic recommendation to replace every wall. The contractor should identify which elevations and components are damaged, check for loose or cracked panels, and look behind the siding where accessible for signs of water getting in.

If replacement is needed, the work generally includes selecting a suitable material and color, removing the affected siding, checking the underlying surface, and installing the new panels with the right trim and details. Ask whether the proposal includes house wrap or other moisture protection if needed, disposal, cleanup, and replacement of damaged trim or accessories.

Does hail damage mean I need to replace all the siding?

Not always. If damage is limited and matching material is available, replacing a section or individual panels may be practical. A full replacement may make more sense when damage is widespread, panels are brittle or difficult to match, or repairs would leave a visibly uneven exterior.

Ask the contractor to show you the damage and explain why each proposed area needs repair or replacement. Hail marks can look concerning, but the decision should account for the siding material, the condition of the panel, and whether it still protects the wall.

What should a local professional check?

A careful inspection should cover more than the most obvious wall. Hail may affect different sides of the house differently, so the contractor should check each elevation, corners, trim, soffits, and other exposed components. They should also note any loose panels, openings, or signs of moisture at seams and around windows.

It can be useful to check nearby roofing and gutters too, since hail can affect more than one part of the exterior. Ask for clear photos and written notes, especially if you plan to discuss the damage with your insurer. The contractor can document what they observed, but the insurer makes its own coverage decision.

What changes the cost of siding replacement?

There is no reliable price without seeing the home and defining the scope. The amount of siding to remove and replace is one factor, but the material, panel profile, color availability, and whether a close match can be found also matter. A repair that requires special-order material may have a different cost and schedule than a straightforward replacement.

  • How many walls, panels, and trim pieces are affected.
  • The siding material and the availability of matching products.
  • Whether damaged siding can be removed without affecting adjacent panels.
  • The condition of the underlying wall and any moisture protection that needs attention.
  • Access to the work area, disposal needs, and any additional exterior repairs included in the proposal.

Ask for an itemized estimate that separates the siding work from optional repairs. If the job involves an insurance claim, compare the contractor's documented scope with the insurer's estimate and ask questions about any differences before work starts.

How long might the work take, and what can delay it?

Timing depends on the size of the project, the material, and whether matching panels are readily available. Small repairs may be simpler to schedule than replacing siding across several elevations, but a contractor should give you a project-specific timeline after inspecting the home.

Material ordering, weather, hidden damage found during removal, and coordination with other exterior work can affect the schedule. Ask when materials are expected, what could change the start date, and how the contractor will handle uncovered wall areas if the work is interrupted.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Agreeing to full replacement without seeing which areas are damaged and why.
  • Choosing a quote by its total alone instead of comparing materials and included work.
  • Assuming new siding will match older panels exactly, especially if the existing product or color is no longer available.
  • Letting work begin before you understand the scope, payment schedule, cleanup, and responsibility for any added repairs.
  • Removing damaged siding before documenting it, if you need photos or an inspection for an insurance discussion.

Get the inspection findings, material choice, and proposed repairs in writing. If a contractor finds damage behind the siding, ask for photos and an updated scope before approving additional work.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Which panels or elevations are damaged, and can you show me the evidence?
  • Would you recommend a targeted repair or broader replacement, and why?
  • Can you match the existing siding, and what will the finished area look like if an exact match is unavailable?
  • What materials, trim, moisture protection, removal, disposal, and cleanup are included?
  • How will you document damage and handle hidden issues found after removal?
  • Who will oversee the work and keep me updated about schedule changes?
  • Do I need to check local permit requirements or HOA rules for this scope?
  • What payment terms and written warranty apply to the work and materials?
